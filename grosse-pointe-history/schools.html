The Schools Saga · 2018–2025

Eight years of Grosse Pointe school politics

One school district, two high schools, five communities — and a decade in which nearly every ordinary governance decision became a proxy for a larger fight.

Reconfiguration and the school closures

The fight has a boring, structural root: for about 15 years, enrollment in the Grosse Pointe Public School System had been falling, and each departing student took roughly $10,000 in state funding with them. By 2019 the district was studying which schools to close.

On June 24, 2019, the Board of Education voted to close Poupard and Trombly elementary schools — the closure vote was 5–2 — and, in a separate 6–1 vote, to reconfigure grades so that all fifth graders would move into the district's three middle schools beginning in 2020–21. Superintendent Gary Niehaus led the process, presenting operational savings and land-value figures for each school under consideration. (The Detroit News; WXYZ)

The community did not take it quietly. Tensions ran high through the spring hearings (Metro Times), and several trustees who supported the plan — including Judy Gafa, Kathleen Abke and Christopher Profeta — were targeted for recall. This is the origin point of the modern factional split: an ordinary demographic problem that hardened into personal, organized opposition.

Why it matters later: the reconfiguration created the first organized "throw them out" coalition in the district — the template that the pandemic, and then national school-board politics, would supercharge. Notably, a later lawsuit alleges that reporter Mary Anne Brush had been investigating whether the Cotton family funded that 2019 recall effort — connecting the very start of the story to figures who dominate its later chapters. See the Brush section.

Brian Summerfield resigns as board president

On January 2, 2020, board president Brian C. Summerfield resigned after nearly seven years on the board, saying the job had become too much of a burden on his family. In his announcement he wrote that his family "could no longer bear the burden of my continued service." His exit came directly on the heels of the closure-and-reconfiguration year. (The Detroit News; WXYZ; Grosse Pointe News)

Documented: Summerfield's stated reason was the toll on his family, coming directly after the closure-and-reconfiguration year. This site does not assert any further motive.

COVID-19 and the fight over reopening

The pandemic shut school buildings in March 2020 — the same stretch in which fifth graders were being moved into the middle schools — and turned every operational decision into a public battle. How quickly to return to in-person instruction, what safety measures to require, and how to weigh learning loss against health risk split the community along lines that increasingly mirrored national partisan divides.

By this period, Jon Dean — long a senior administrator in the district — was the central administrative figure and would soon become superintendent. The reopening period is also when the district, like much of Michigan and the country, lost students to private and parochial schools, deepening the enrollment problem that had started the whole cycle.

The mask fight

As buildings reopened, masking became the flashpoint. Public-comment periods at board meetings filled with parents on both sides, and the mask-versus-no-mask argument became the recruiting ground for the slate of candidates who would reshape the board in November 2022. Grosse Pointe was one of many Michigan districts where these meetings turned into what the Detroit News called "emotional battlegrounds."

2022: the board turns over

In November 2022, the accumulated anger found an outlet at the ballot box. Sean Cotton — a wealthy Grosse Pointe Farms businessman who by then owned the Grosse Pointe News — was the top vote-getter, joining the board alongside Valarie St. John and Ginny Jeup. (C&G Newspapers) The new configuration produced a conservative-leaning majority, with Ahmed Ismail as president and Lisa Papas as vice president.

The local race sat inside a national story: groups like Moms for Liberty were pushing organized slates into suburban school boards across the country, and Michigan was a focus. (Michigan Advance; Michigan Public) Jeup's seat came down to a recount decided by four votes — see National Threads for her recount and her presence at the U.S. Capitol on January 6.

The Grosse Pointe North health clinic — halted 4–3

The new majority's most consequential early act came fast. On January 19, 2023, at a special meeting, the board voted 4–3 to halt work and spending on a free youth health clinic planned for Grosse Pointe North High School.

Voting to stop: Ahmed Ismail, Sean Cotton, Ginny Jeup, and Lisa Papas. Voting to continue: David Brumbaugh, Valarie St. John, and Colleen Worden. (The Detroit News; C&G Newspapers; Michigan Public)

The clinic would have been open to anyone ages 5–21 from the Pointes and Harper Woods, offering free physical and mental-health services. It would have cost the district about $1 million in construction from the sinking fund, with operating costs covered by state and other grants. Because the state grant required the facility to be running by fall 2023, Superintendent Jon Dean confirmed the "pause" effectively killed the project. (ClickOnDetroit)

The stated objection was that tax dollars shouldn't be used for the clinic; supporters argued the sinking fund and grants could lawfully cover it — the funding dispute is examined in South vs North. The decision drew national notice. (The Mary Sue)

The communications director is cut — then sues

Mary Anne Brush spent about five years as a reporter at the Grosse Pointe News — where, according to her lawsuit, she had investigated whether the Cotton family was funding the 2019 recall effort against three board members. After Sean Cotton acquired the paper, she left and, in February 2021, became the communications director for the school district. In the summer of 2023, her position was eliminated in budget cuts.

According to her federal lawsuit, when the board needed to cut roughly $4 million, the majority — Ismail, Papas, Cotton and Jeup — bypassed the administration's recommendations and passed a board-developed budget that eliminated her job, despite strong performance reviews. Brush sued the district and board members for wrongful termination over her perceived political beliefs, alleging violations of her First and Fourteenth Amendment rights. The case, Brush v. Grosse Pointe Public School System (No. 4:24-cv-11495), is in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan and is a matter of public record. (Pitt McGehee Palmer Bonanni & Rivers; Justia docket; CourtListener)

Jon Dean's departure and the retaliation report

On August 23, 2023, two weeks before the school year, the board voted 4–2 to accept Superintendent Jon Dean's "retirement." In favor: Ismail, Papas, Cotton and Jeup. Opposed: Worden and St. John. Ismail said Dean came to the board with the proposal; many parents did not believe it, coming as it did amid months of nine-hour meetings, picketing teachers, and an exodus of senior central-office staff. (The Detroit News; C&G Newspapers)

"It is irresponsible to let a highly effective superintendent … be driven out. Especially two weeks before school." — Trustee Colleen Worden, voting against accepting Dean's retirement (Detroit News)

What "driven out" meant became clearer in 2025, when the board voted to release the full outside investigation into a retaliation complaint. Reporting on the report describes a witness statement that Ismail said the board was moving the superintendent out and would "go after" complainants next. (The Detroit News; The Tower Pulse)

The report, the retaliation finding against board president Ahmed Ismail, the exodus of North administrators, and the board-driven gutting of central office are covered in full on the Administration page.

Framing note: the official action was a voluntary "retirement." The characterization that Dean was forced out is Worden's on-the-record statement and is supported by the later investigation's witness accounts — but it remains a contested characterization, which is exactly why the vote counts and the report matter.

2024: voters reject the Cotton-backed slate

In November 2024, the reaction arrived. A four-candidate slate — Colleen Worden (reelected), Tim Klepp, Laura Hull and Clint Derringer — defeated the four-person slate backed by board president Sean Cotton, flipping the board's majority. (The Detroit News; C&G Newspapers)

The losing side was heavily resourced: Cotton put $441,000 into a super PAC backing his slate — board member Terry Collins, Kate Elizabeth Hopper, Melissa Fradenburg and Heath Marshall Roberts — alongside billboards along I-94 and a community newspaper owned by the sitting board president. (Detroit Free Press opinion, via Yahoo) The full accounting of that spending and Cotton's overlapping roles is in The Cotton Era.
