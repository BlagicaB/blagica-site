The Cotton Era

A billionaire, a newspaper, and a school board

The Cotton family helped build up Grosse Pointe Park. This page is not about that legacy — it's a neutral look at one narrower, documented question: what happens when one wealthy person simultaneously owns the town paper, sits on the school board, and funds education nonprofits.

First, the fair context, stated plainly: the Cotton family has invested heavily in the Pointes — notably the retail strip in Grosse Pointe Park — and their money has visibly improved parts of the community. That is real, and residents who admire it are not wrong to. The material below is not a rebuttal to that; it is a separate accounting of roles and their overlaps.

Buying the Grosse Pointe News

Sean Cotton bought the Grosse Pointe News in 2021, framing the purchase as a rescue of a struggling community paper. (Crain's Detroit Business) The paper is the dominant local outlet in a town where local coverage is otherwise thin — a fact that becomes significant once its owner is also a candidate for, and then president of, the school board.

One early, concrete consequence: Mary Anne Brush, a reporter at the paper for about five years, left after the sale and became the school district's communications director — a job she later lost under the Cotton-aligned board majority, and over which she is now suing. See the Brush firing and lawsuit.

Stepping down as publisher — and running for the board

Cotton stepped down as the paper's publisher shortly before registering as a school-board candidate, though he continued to own the paper. (Grosse Pointe News) In November 2022 he was the top vote-getter, and he went on to serve in leadership roles (treasurer, then president) on the board that halted the North clinic and accepted Superintendent Jon Dean's departure. (C&G Newspapers)

The structural point (neutral): a sitting school-board member who owns the only substantial local newspaper controls both a governing vote and the town's primary channel for covering that government. Whatever any individual's intentions, that is a concentration of local informational and political power worth documenting on its own terms.

Pointers for Academic Excellence (a 501(c)(4))

Shortly after his 2022 election, Cotton started Pointers for Academic Excellence (PfAE). The Grosse Pointe News reported the group named Kevin Nugent, a Grosse Pointe South graduate, as executive director, and quoted Cotton saying he is not actively involved in running it. (Grosse Pointe News) A related committee appears in Michigan campaign-finance records. (Transparency USA)

If PfAE is organized as a 501(c)(4) "social welfare" nonprofit — a point to confirm from its filing — such entities are not required to disclose their donors, which is why they're often called "dark money." The relevant, neutral fact here is the combination: the group was founded by a sitting board member who also owns the local paper.

Where to verify: "Pointers for Academic Excellence" is registered in Michigan and can be looked up in the LARA Corporations Division entity search (the group's own site is pf-ae.org). A separately named super PAC — "Grosse Pointe Residents for Academic Excellence" — appears in campaign-finance records. Confirm the exact entity type from the LARA filing before stating a specific 501(c) designation.

The STEAM Foundation

A STEAM Foundation was stood up with Cotton's backing while he was on the board. His sister-in-law Jennifer Kendall was involved at its founding and spoke at a public GPPSS board meeting when the foundation was introduced — putting the entity on the public record from the start. It stands as a second Cotton-connected education entity operating alongside the district he helped govern.

The 2024 campaign spending

In the 2024 school-board race, Cotton put $441,000 into a super PAC to back his slate — board member Terry Collins, Kate Elizabeth Hopper, Melissa Fradenburg and Heath Marshall Roberts — with billboards along I-94. The slate lost to the four-candidate reform slate of Worden, Klepp, Hull and Derringer. (Detroit Free Press opinion; Detroit News)

On the number: $441,000 is the reported super-PAC figure. Cotton's total election-related spending across cycles is a matter of public campaign-finance record; linking the Michigan/Wayne County filing directly would cite the primary source rather than the news account.

Concentrated influence — stated neutrally

Set the individual disputes aside and list only the roles, each of which is documented above:

  • Owner of the dominant local newspaper.
  • Elected school-board member and, for a period, board president.
  • Founder of an affiliated education nonprofit/committee (PfAE).
  • Reported funder of a second education entity (STEAM Foundation — ⚠ needs source).
  • A self-funded campaign apparatus in board elections.

Reasonable people disagree about what to make of this. Supporters see a civic-minded resident using his resources to help local schools. Critics see an unusual concentration of money, media and governing power in one household in a town of a few thousand voters. This site's position is only that the combination is real and documented, and that voters heading into the next election are entitled to see it laid out plainly.
