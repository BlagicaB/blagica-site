The Schools & the Community

The other side of the ledger

The disputes catalogued on this site are real — but so is the thing everyone is fighting over: a genuinely strong school district, and an unusually engaged community. Here are the numbers and the groups, sourced.

It matters that the fights described elsewhere played out at a district that was, by standard measures, succeeding. That's part of why they were so bitter — the stakes were something valuable.

By the numbers

Grosse Pointe Public Schools serves roughly 6,500 students across 12 schools (seven elementary, three middle, two high). On standard state measures it sits near the top of Michigan:

  • District math proficiency ~54% vs. a Michigan average around 34%; reading ~64% vs. ~46%.
  • Graduation rate ~96%, well above the state's ~81%.
  • Niche grades the district A+ and ranks it #2 in Wayne County; SchoolDigger gives it 5 stars and ranks it in the top ~7% of Michigan districts.

(U.S. News; Niche; SchoolDigger; Public School Review)

The two high schools

Grosse Pointe South — U.S. News ranks it roughly #9 in Michigan: about 80% reading and 63% math proficiency, a 97% graduation rate, and 69% AP participation. (U.S. News)

Grosse Pointe North — a 95% graduation rate and about 70% reading proficiency, with math proficiency (~38%) closer to the state average. (U.S. News)

Honest note: both schools clear state averages comfortably, but the South–North gap is real and is examined on the South vs North page. Rankings also vary by source and year — the figures above are the latest published by each rater, linked so you can check them.

The advocates

Grosse Pointe's civic engagement is not only the conflict that makes news. Several organized groups work, in different ways, on behalf of the public schools and the broader community.

Alliance for GP Public Schools

A grassroots, volunteer group of Grosse Pointe and Harper Woods residents that describes itself as a 501(c)(4) organized around "pro-student, pro-teacher, pro-public education" advocacy. It attends board and committee meetings, presses for transparency and accountability, and — after interviews and research — endorses school-board candidates who match its values. The group says it has no benefactors financing it and relies on small fundraising and grants. (alliancegpps.org)

WE GP — Welcoming Everyone Grosse Pointe

A community organization focused on civil and human rights, inclusion, and community engagement — broader than schools alone. It runs social and civic programming, including Grosse Pointe Pride, and mobilizes residents around local issues. (wegp.org)

Grosse Pointe Foundation for Public Education

A nonpartisan nonprofit that raises money to fund grants and enhancements across the district — the least political of the three, and worth naming because not all civic activity here is a fight. (gpfpe.org)

The pushback

These groups are not universally embraced, and neutrality means saying so. A vocal set of residents — overlapping with the Cotton-aligned board faction and with national movements like Moms for Liberty — views organizations such as the Alliance and WE GP as partisan or activist rather than neutral community groups, and has organized its own efforts. Examples include the Eastside Republican Club's "Protect Our Grosse Pointe Schools" campaigning and coverage in conservative outlets such as Michigan Capitol Confidential. (Eastside Republican Club; Michigan Capitol Confidential)

Both camps claim to be acting "for the kids." That is the actual shape of the disagreement — not good residents versus bad ones, but two organized visions of what the schools should be, competing at the ballot box and in the board room.

Framing note: calling any of these groups "partisan" is itself a contested characterization. Judge each on its own materials, membership, and filings — several are linked above — rather than on how opponents describe them.
